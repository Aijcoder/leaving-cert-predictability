The finding

Frequency pays. Rotation doesn't.

Horizontal: how much of the course you revised. Vertical: the share of the paper you could have attempted, counting the paper's own choice rules. Averaged over the years tested.

Going from picking topics at random to picking the ones that come up most often is worth about nine marks in every hundred. Adding a rule that chases topics which have been away a while is worth about half a mark — and that half mark cannot be separated from noise.

On Maths the two upper curves sit on top of each other exactly. That is not a missing line; it is the result.

Breakdown

Where those marks come from

Start from the worst case — every question compulsory, topics picked at random — and add one advantage at a time, for a student who revised half the course.

The largest single advantage is not knowledge at all — it is the structure of the paper. Being allowed to choose your questions is worth about twenty marks per hundred in Physics, and it is free.

Method

How this was measured

The full code and reports are in the repository; this is the shape of it.

Split the papers

Each paper cut into its smallest marked pieces, with marks taken from the official marking scheme. All 74 papers add back up to their printed totals — that is the check that the splitting worked.

Record what you may skip

"Answer any five of eight" changes everything for a partly-revised student, so every paper's rubric was parsed into a structure the program scores against.

Freeze the topic lists

Topics taken from the syllabus headings themselves, then fingerprinted before any labelling, so the categories could not be reshaped once results appeared.

Label every part

Each part read with its marking-scheme steps; every scoring step assigned a topic. Weights come from the marks the scheme awards, computed in code. 3,546 of 3,549 parts got a valid label.

Score without hindsight

For each year, a method sees only earlier years. This is enforced in the program: reaching for its own year or a later one raises an error and the run stops.

Test the tester first

Before any real paper was touched, the whole machine ran 4,000 times on synthetic papers with a known truth. It had to report a false discovery no more than 8% of the time when there was nothing there, and find a planted pattern at least 80% of the time. Those controls caught two real defects before any result existed.

Labels came from a language model (deepseek-v4-1-flash-260910) reading each part with its marking scheme. Relabelling a 10% sample from scratch gives the same main topic 91–100% of the time. They have not been checked against a teacher's judgement on a blind sample.

Before you use it

What this cannot tell you

The tempting misuse of this page is to read the topic table as a tip sheet. Half of that is supported.

"Heat capacity appeared on all 25 papers" is a fact you can act on. "Radioactivity is due this year" is not, however the table is sorted. That claim was tested directly and failed.
The State Examinations Commission sets the papers, not a pattern. Syllabuses change, examiners change, and nothing obliges the next paper to resemble the last 25.
Only Physics carries a confirmed result. It has 25 sittings. Maths has 13 on the current syllabus, so its numbers are exploratory. Applied Maths has 4 and is described but never analysed.
"No evidence" is not "proven absent". It means any timing effect is too small for this much data to detect — and small enough to be worth under half a mark in a hundred.
The 2020–2022 papers carried Covid adjustments and the 2020 papers' month could not be determined. Excluding those years changes nothing important.

Papers and marking schemes are published by the State Examinations Commission. Nothing on this page reproduces them: it holds statistics, topic labels and links. To read a question, use the SEC's own archive.
